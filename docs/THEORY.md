# Theory notes

A compact map of the ideas behind each module, what the validation suite checks, and where to read more.

## Random numbers and variates (`rng`, `rngtests`, `special`)

A linear congruential generator x_{k+1} = (a x_k + c) mod m has full period m exactly under the Hull-Dobell
conditions; every LCG's d-tuples lie on a lattice (Marsaglia 1968), and the spectral test measures the distance
between its hyperplanes (RANDU's triples lie on 15 planes). Modern generators (xorshift-family, PCG, NumPy's
PCG64) add non-linear output permutations to a large state. Statistical tests (chi-square, Kolmogorov-Smirnov,
serial, runs, gap) can only reject; second-level tests check that their p-values are uniform. Inversion gives
F⁻¹(U) ~ F; acceptance-rejection accepts Y ~ g with probability f(Y)/(Mg(Y)) at mean cost M; Box-Muller,
Marsaglia's polar method and Kinderman-Monahan's ratio of uniforms produce exact normals; Walker's alias method
samples a discrete law in O(1) after Vose's O(n) set-up; Marsaglia and Tsang's squeeze generates gammas. The
special functions follow Wichura's AS 241 and the continued fractions of Numerical Recipes. *Knuth, TAOCP vol. 2
(1997); Devroye, Non-Uniform Random Variate Generation (1986); L'Ecuyer & Simard, TestU01 (2007); O'Neill, PCG (2014).*

## Monte Carlo, variance reduction and quasi-Monte Carlo (`montecarlo`, `qmc`)

The sample mean has standard error σ/√n in any dimension; t intervals are exact only for normal outputs and
undercover for skewed or heavy-tailed ones; Wilson's interval stays valid for rare events, whose relative error
under crude Monte Carlo is √((1 − p)/(np)). Antithetic pairs help when h is monotone; a control variate with
known mean reduces the variance by 1/(1 − R²); importance sampling reweights by f/g and needs light-tailed
weights (exponential tilting to the dominating point for normal tails); stratification removes between-strata
variance (Neyman allocation n_j ∝ p_j σ_j); common random numbers sharpen comparisons. Low-discrepancy sequences
(Halton, Sobol with Joe-Kuo direction numbers, rank-1 lattices) bound the error by variation × discrepancy
(Koksma-Hlawka); random shifts restore unbiasedness and error estimates. *Glasserman, Monte Carlo Methods in
Financial Engineering (2003); Owen, Monte Carlo theory, methods and examples (2013); Dick & Pillichshammer (2010).*

## Markov chains, random walks and branching (`markov`, `walks`)

A finite chain's communicating classes are the strongly connected components of its graph; closed classes are
recurrent, open ones transient; the period is the gcd of cycle lengths. An irreducible chain has a unique
stationary law π = πP; Kac's lemma gives mean return times 1/π_i, and the fundamental matrix
Z = (I − P + 1π)⁻¹ gives mean first-passage times and Kemeny's constant. Total-variation distance decays at the
rate of the second eigenvalue (the spectral gap), with cutoff phenomena such as the Ehrenfest urn's
½N log N. Reversibility (detailed balance) implies a real spectrum. Absorbing chains are solved by N = (I − Q)⁻¹.
The reflection principle gives the law of the maximum of a walk and the ballot theorem; Lévy's arcsine laws
describe time spent ahead and the last zero; Pólya's theorem separates recurrence (d ≤ 2) from transience
(d ≥ 3). A Galton-Watson process dies out with probability equal to the smallest fixed point of its offspring
pgf. *Norris, Markov Chains (1997); Levin, Peres & Wilmer, Markov Chains and Mixing Times (2017); Feller vol. 1
(1968); Athreya & Ney, Branching Processes (1972).*

## Poisson and renewal processes (`poisson`, `renewal`)

A Poisson process has independent Poisson increments, exponential gaps and uniformly scattered points given
their number; non-homogeneous processes are generated by Lewis-Shedler thinning or by time change through the
cumulative intensity; marking splits a Poisson process into independent Poisson processes; a compound Poisson
sum has variance λtE[Y²]. Exact (Garwood) intervals for rates come from chi-square quantiles; the index of
dispersion tests Poisson counts. A renewal process's mean count solves the renewal equation m = F + F * m; the
elementary and key renewal theorems give m(t) ~ t/μ + (σ² − μ²)/(2μ²). Length-biased sampling makes the
interval covering a fixed time have mean E[X²]/E[X] (the inspection paradox); the renewal-reward theorem prices
maintenance policies; alternating renewal availability is insensitive to the distributions. *Ross, Stochastic
Processes (1996); Kingman, Poisson Processes (1993); Cox, Renewal Theory (1962).*

## Continuous-time chains, queues and discrete-event simulation (`ctmc`, `queues`, `des`)

A CTMC with generator Q has P(t) = e^{Qt}, computed by scaling and squaring with a Padé approximant or by
uniformisation (a Poisson mixture of powers of I + Q/Λ with a known truncation error); its MLE is transition
counts over occupation times. Birth-death chains satisfy detailed balance, giving the M/M/c/K laws; Erlang B
(by the stable recursion) and Erlang C give blocking and waiting probabilities; Pollaczek-Khinchine gives M/G/1
waits proportional to (1 + c_s²)/2; Kingman and Allen-Cunneen approximate G/G/c; Little's law L = λW needs no
distributional assumptions; square-root staffing (Halfin-Whitt) describes large call centres; Jackson networks
have product-form laws. Gillespie's direct method simulates reaction networks exactly; tau-leaping approximates
it. Event-scheduling simulation advances a clock through a priority queue of future events. *Kleinrock,
Queueing Systems (1975); Gross & Harris (2008); Gans, Koole & Mandelbaum, call-centre survey (2003); Gillespie
(1977); Law, Simulation Modeling and Analysis (2015).*

## Output analysis (`output`)

Simulation outputs are autocorrelated: Var(x̄) ≈ τσ²/n with the integrated autocorrelation time τ, estimated
with Sokal's self-consistent window or Geyer's initial positive sequence. Batch means, independent replications
and the regenerative method (ratio of i.i.d. cycle sums, delta-method variance) give valid intervals; Welch's
moving average and MSER-5 choose the warm-up; Gelman-Rubin's R-hat compares chains started apart. *Alexopoulos &
Seila (1998); Sokal, Monte Carlo methods in statistical mechanics (1997); Gelman & Rubin (1992); White (1997).*

## Brownian motion and SDEs (`brownian`, `sde`)

Brownian motion is the Gaussian process with covariance min(s, t): Lévy's midpoint construction refines a path,
Donsker's theorem makes it the limit of scaled walks, its quadratic variation is t and its total variation
infinite. The reflection principle gives P(max ≥ a) = 2P(W_t ≥ a); first-passage times are inverse Gaussian with
drift; the Brownian-bridge crossing probability exp(−2(a − x₀)(a − x₁)/(σ²Δt)) corrects discretely monitored
barriers (Merton's formula is the continuous reference). Itô's lemma solves GBM exactly; the OU process is
sampled exactly as an AR(1). Euler-Maruyama has strong order ½ and weak order 1, Milstein strong order 1;
stochastic Heun converges to the Stratonovich solution, which differs from Itô's by the drift ½bb′. CIR needs
full truncation; multilevel Monte Carlo (Giles 2008) reduces the cost of expectations to O(ε⁻²(log ε)²).
*Kloeden & Platen (1992); Higham, SIAM Review (2001); Karatzas & Shreve (1991); Giles, Acta Numerica (2015).*

## Gaussian processes and time series (`gaussian`, `timeseries`)

Covariance functions set path smoothness (exponential, Matérn, squared exponential); Cholesky sampling costs
O(n³) and fails numerically for smooth kernels; circulant embedding samples stationary processes on grids by FFT
when the embedding is non-negative definite (Dietrich & Newsam 1997; Wood & Chan 1994), always for fractional
Gaussian noise (Davies-Harte). GP conditioning is linear algebra with a closed-form marginal likelihood
(Rasmussen & Williams 2006). ARMA processes are causal and invertible when their polynomials' roots lie outside
the unit circle; the Durbin-Levinson recursion gives Yule-Walker estimates and the PACF; Ljung-Box tests
residual whiteness; the periodogram is inconsistent and Welch's averaging fixes it. *Brockwell & Davis, Time
Series: Theory and Methods (1991); Lord, Powell & Shardlow (2014).*

## Markov chain Monte Carlo (`mcmc`)

Metropolis-Hastings builds a chain reversible with respect to the target; random-walk proposals are optimally
scaled at 2.38/√d with acceptance 0.234 (Roberts, Gelman & Gilks 1997); Gibbs sampling updates full conditionals
and mixes slowly on correlated targets; Hamiltonian Monte Carlo follows leapfrog trajectories (second order,
symplectic) and accepts on the energy error (Duane et al. 1987; Neal 2011). The 2-D Ising model has an exact
solution (Onsager 1944; Yang 1952) and a critical point at β_c = ½ln(1 + √2) where local samplers slow down;
simulated annealing applies Metropolis at a falling temperature. *Robert & Casella, Monte Carlo Statistical
Methods (2004); Brooks et al., Handbook of MCMC (2011); Newman & Barkema (1999).*
